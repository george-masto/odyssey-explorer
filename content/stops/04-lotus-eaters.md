tidbits:
  - text: "What plant was it? Herodotus, placing lotus-eating peoples on this same North African coast, describes a fruit sweet as the date; Polybius describes the Libyan jujube (Ziziphus lotus), whose berries were fermented into wine; moderns have added dates, hackberries, and outright opiates. Every identification is conjecture — the fruit grows reliably only in the poem."
    source: "Hdt. 4.177–178; Polybius, in Athenaeus 14.651 — identifications conjectural"
  - text: "Djerba's claim is ancient, not a tourist-board invention: when a Roman fleet ran aground there in 253 BC, Polybius calls the island 'the island of the Lotus-eaters', and Strabo repeats the identification of Meninx — Djerba's ancient name — with Homer's lotus-land."
    source: "Polybius 1.39.2; Strabo 17.3.17"
  - text: "The Lotus-eaters 'did not plan death for our comrades' — they are the only adversaries in the wanderings who harm no one. The episode inverts the poem's great theme of hospitality: a host can destroy you simply by making you want to stay. Note who uses force here — Odysseus, dragging his weeping men to the benches and tying them down."
    source: "Od. 9.92–99"
  - text: "Tennyson's The Lotos-Eaters (1832) grows Homer's brief episode into a full choric dream — 'In the afternoon they came unto a land / In which it seemed always afternoon' — and made 'lotus-eater' the English word for blissful truancy. The poem in turn inspired Robert Duncanson's Land of the Lotus Eaters, this stop's artwork, which the painter reportedly showed to Tennyson himself in 1863."
    source: "Tennyson, The Lotos-Eaters (1832); Ketner, The Emergence of the African-American Artist (1993)"
