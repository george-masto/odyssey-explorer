tidbits:
  - text: "The sack holds no treasure: Aeolus has bottled every wind that could blow the fleet off course, leaving only a steady West Wind free to carry it home, and lashed the oxhide shut with a cord of silver. Nine days Odysseus steers without handing off the sheet; in sight of Ithaca he finally sleeps, and the crew — certain the bag hides gold and silver they have been denied a share of — untie it. All the winds leap out, and the storm carries them the entire way back."
    source: "Od. 10.19–26, 10.34–55"
  - text: "The second visit is the coldest door-slam in the poem. Aeolus refuses even to hear the case out: 'Begone from our island with speed, thou vilest of all that live' — for a man blown back onto your doorstep is a man the blessed gods hate, and helping him is not θέμις, not sanctioned. Odysseus is sent out groaning, and this time there is no wind at all: the crossing to the Laestrygonians is rowed."
    source: "Od. 10.72–79"
  - text: "Strabo records the tradition that made Strongyle — 'the Round One', today's Stromboli — the seat of Aeolus, steward of the winds. The cone has been in near-continuous mild eruption throughout recorded history, flaring above the night sea every few minutes, and sailors' usage crowned it the lighthouse of the Tyrrhenian."
    source: "Strabo, Geography 6.2.11; the lighthouse epithet is modern maritime tradition"
  - text: "Homer's Aeolia is a floating island ringed by 'a wall of unbreakable bronze' on sheer rock. Readers ancient and modern have heard a volcanic seamark in that description — an island that glows, shimmers on the horizon haze, and could never be walled by human hands — and the whole Lipari arc duly took, and keeps, the name Aeolian."
    source: "Od. 10.1–4"
