  en: "As soon as early Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered, then I sent forth my comrades to the house of Circe to fetch the body of the dead Elpenor. Straightway then we cut billets of wood and gave him burial where the headland runs furthest out to sea, sorrowing and shedding big tears. But when the dead man was burned, and the armour of the dead, we heaped up a mound and dragged on to it a pillar, and on the top of the mound we planted his shapely oar."

tidbits:
  - text: "Elpenor's ghost is the first shade Odysseus meets in Hades — ahead of even Tiresias — because he is unburied: his body still lies in Circe's hall 'unwept and unburied', and the unburied dead wait at the door. His plea ends with a threat older than the poem: neglect me, and I become 'a cause of the gods' wrath' against you."
    source: "Od. 11.51–80"
  - text: "His death was farce, not battle: heavy with wine he had climbed to Circe's roof for cool air, woke at the bustle of departure, forgot the ladder, and stepped off — the neck snapped from the spine, and his soul went down to Hades. The Odyssey's first casualty of Book 11 is a workplace accident."
    source: "Od. 10.552–560"
  - text: "The oar is granted verbatim: in Hades Elpenor begged for a mound at the grey sea's edge, 'a marker for a man of misfortune, for men to come to learn of', with the oar planted on top — the one he pulled in life among his comrades. A sailor's headstone: the tool as the man."
    source: "Od. 11.75–78, 12.15"
  - text: "Circe hails the returning crew as δισθανέες — 'twice-dying men', who will owe death two visits while everyone else pays once. Then her second briefing maps every danger to come — Sirens, the Clashing Rocks, Scylla and Charybdis, Thrinacia — so Book 12 sails through a route the audience has already read."
    source: "Od. 12.21–22, 12.37–141"

now_today: "Monte Circeo again, from the seaward side this time: the 541-metre limestone massif rises sheer out of a dead-flat coastal plain, and from a deck offshore it reads unmistakably as an island — the visual root of the Aeaea tradition. Locals still read the mountain's profile from the north as a woman's upturned face, la Maga Circe; below it, San Felice Circeo and the national park keep the sorceress's name on the map."
